\begin{abstract}
Zstandard's dictionary trainer (COVER/fastCOVER, WWW 2016)~\cite{liao2016cover}
is the de-facto standard for small-record compression, deployed in
RocksDB, ScyllaDB, and the emerging shared-dictionary web
ecosystem~\cite{rfc9842} -- yet it has received no algorithmic attention
since 2018 despite documented pathological failures. We present the
first mechanistic diagnosis of its behavior on real corpora, identifying
three defects: (1) \emph{budget degeneracy} -- past internal caps,
requesting a larger dictionary silently yields a smaller one, collapsing
compression ratio by up to 31\% on zstd's own benchmark corpus, a
signature consistent with an unexplained sensitivity a production
engineer reported upstream (a 512\,KB budget yielding a $90\times$ ratio
against 550\,KB yielding $14\times$ on their data), in a thread that was
answered and closed without the underlying trainer behavior
changing~\cite{zstdissue4127}; (2) \emph{size blindness} -- the trainer
never searches dictionary size, though the optimal size varies by two
orders of magnitude across corpora and the default 110\,KB budget leaves
up to 22\% compression on the table; (3) \emph{level blindness} --
construction decisions (content selection, size response, repcode
seeding) have opposite effects at fast versus high compression levels,
yet the trainer targets a single level. Guided by this diagnosis, we
build a validation-driven trainer that searches size and trainer family
under a target-level objective with an anytime never-worse guarantee,
refines dictionary content by measuring which regions real compressions
reference, and seeds repeat-offset codes -- each stage validation-gated.
On five real corpora spanning JSON records, event streams, server logs,
and tabular data, our trainer improves compression ratio over
\texttt{zstd --train} defaults by 3.1--14.3\% at level 3 and
11.5--32.1\% at level 19 (heldout, single-shot), beats matched-size
rebuilds of the incumbent trainer at Cassandra's 64\,KB production
budget and at a level-1 evaluation setting motivated by ScyllaDB's RPC
compression path (off by default), and beats an equal-wall-clock sweep
of the incumbent optimizer on four of five corpora at level 19. Output is 100\%-standard-format, usable by every
deployed zstd. Tool and all experiments are open source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Dictionaries are how zstd compresses small data, and small data is
where modern systems live: key-value stores, logs, telemetry, and the
emerging shared-dictionary web transport~\cite{rfc9842}. One trainer
serves all of it; it is a 2016 heuristic~\cite{liao2016cover}, frozen
since 2018, whose maintainer states that the ideal dictionary builder
``does not exist.''

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The first mechanistic quantitative diagnosis of the incumbent
    trainer, tracing the budget-degeneracy collapse to its selection
    objective, quantifying size blindness, and confirming level
    blindness three independent ways; the budget-degeneracy finding is
    consistent with a sensitivity collapse reported upstream but never
    explained~\cite{zstdissue4127}.
  \item A level-aware, validation-driven trainer with an anytime-safety
    guarantee (Propositions~\ref{prop:budget-monotonicity}
    and~\ref{prop:anytime-floor}), each stage individually gated and
    validation-checked.
  \item A 5-corpus, 2-level benchmark against the deployed default,
    a klauspost \texttt{builddict}~\cite{klauspost} baseline, an
    equal-wall-clock stock sweep, a no-training control, and matched-size
    rebuilds at Cassandra's 64\,KB production budget, RocksDB's opt-in
    no-trainer fallback, and a level-1 evaluation setting motivated by
    (but not identical to) ScyllaDB's off-by-default RPC path
    (\S\ref{sec:background-deployment}), isolating how much of the
    headline gain is size versus method.
  \item An open-source tool that emits 100\%-standard-format
    dictionaries, requiring no change to any zstd library or client.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Dictionaries, indexing, and the trainer}
\label{sec:background-zstd-dicts}

A zstd dictionary is a self-describing binary artifact -- magic number,
ID, entropy tables (12 bytes of seeded recent-offset values), then
\texttt{Content}, a virtual ``past'' matches may reference by
offset~\cite{rfc8878}. Both loaders parse this independently and
symmetrically, so a dictionary from any compliant writer is a drop-in
replacement for \texttt{zstd --train}: \emph{fact 1}. \texttt{Content}
is not indexed uniformly, though: fast strategies hash it into one or
two tables where later positions can overwrite earlier ones, while
high-level binary-tree strategies retain every position -- how much of
``the dictionary'' is reachable is a function of level: \emph{fact 2},
the mechanism behind level blindness (\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-level-blindness}).

The trainer solves a covering problem: COVER~\cite{liao2016cover} builds
an exact suffix array over $d$-length substrings and scores each by
document frequency, deduplicated per sample (a dictionary only helps the
\emph{first} reference); fastCOVER trades this for a lossy, streaming
hash-table count. Both partition the corpus into epochs, greedily pack
the best-scoring $k$-byte window per epoch into the output buffer back
forward (strongest content nearest the data, cheapest to reference), and
grid-search $(d,k)$, keeping whichever candidate, once finalized,
achieves the smallest \emph{measured} check-set compressed size --
selection is literal compression, not a proxy. \texttt{zstd --train}
with no flags, and the zero-configuration
\texttt{ZDICT\_trainFromBuffer} used by every downstream integration we
examine, both hard-code fastCOVER-optimize at $d{=}8$, scored at level
3, \texttt{--maxdict} defaulting to 110\,KB. Two paths never run:
repeat-offset seeding is computed and discarded behind \texttt{\#if 0}
(evaluated in \S\ref{sec:design-stage3}), and the size-response
\texttt{shrinkDict} path is hard-coded off (diagnosed in
\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-size-blindness}). All claims here were re-checked
at file:line precision against \texttt{facebook/zstd main@82d322c}
(v1.6.0); we cite specific lines in \S\ref{sec:diagnosis} where one is
load-bearing.

\subsection{Deployment reality}
\label{sec:background-deployment}
\begin{sloppypar}
Every production system we compare against calls
\texttt{ZDICT\_trainFromBuffer} above, or, with no trainer,
\texttt{ZDICT\_finalizeDictionary}, which skips content selection and
attaches only the format header around caller-supplied (often raw)
content. \textbf{RocksDB's} dictionary path is opt-in and off by
default (\texttt{CompressionOptions::\-max\_dict\_bytes}$=0$); once
enabled, \texttt{use\_\-zstd\_\-dict\_\-trainer}$=$\texttt{true} picks our
trainer path by default, at 110\,KB, level 3
(\texttt{compression\_type.h}:238,302, RocksDB \texttt{v11.8.1}) -- so
Experiment~D (\S\ref{sec:evaluation-parity}) tests an opt-in,
non-default path. \textbf{ScyllaDB} trains RPC and SSTable dictionaries through the same
110\,KiB \texttt{ZDICT\_trainFromBuffer} call
(\texttt{dict\_trainer.hh}:76),
but only one is deployed by default: RPC training defaults to off
(\texttt{rpc\_\-dict\_\-training\_\-when}$=$\texttt{NEVER}), so our level-1
cell is a setting \emph{motivated by} that path, not a production
claim; SSTable dictionaries, by contrast, we understated -- available
since release 2025.2, and (commit \texttt{adf9c42}, 2025-10-31)
ScyllaDB's \emph{default} SSTable compressor since 2025.4, so the
110\,KiB/level-3 configuration we already test as the deployed default
(Table~\ref{tab:t1-main}) is production-representative there.
\textbf{Cassandra's} CEP-54 is not merged or shipped: tracking epic
CASSANDRA-20902 is \texttt{In Progress}, fix version \texttt{7.x}; its
code lives only on the unreleased \texttt{cassandra-6.0} branch
(\texttt{6.0-alpha2}; the latest release is 5.0.x), and CASSANDRA-21154
removed its auto-training as explicit ``dead code.'' We keep the
64\,KiB figure as the code's own default
(\texttt{maxDictionarySize}$=65536$,
\texttt{Compression\-Dictionary\-Training\-Config.java}), though CEP-54's
design document itself recommends ``around 100KiB,'' closer to the
110\,KiB figure used elsewhere. We use these budgets and levels as
evaluation targets in \S\ref{sec:evaluation-parity}, each labeled by
what it is -- shipped default, evaluation setting motivated by an
off-by-default path, or unreleased branch -- not uniformly
``production.''
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Diagnosis (measurements on real corpora)}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

\subsection{Budget degeneracy (the ``cliff'')}
\label{sec:diagnosis-cliff}

Consider a user who has trained a dictionary with \texttt{zstd
--train-cover} and raises \texttt{--maxdict} for a better one. On
\texttt{github\_users} -- zstd's own published benchmark sample set --
this produces Figure~\ref{fig:f1-cliff}'s step function: at requests of
262--524\,KiB the trainer emits exactly what was asked and ratio holds
near 10; past 1\,MiB it emits only $\sim$415--425\,KiB regardless of how
much more is requested, and ratio falls to 7.49 (a 24\% loss) and then
6.95 (31\%) -- \emph{smaller output at a larger request}, with no
warning at any verbosity level. The symptom is not new; what is new is
the explanation. In August 2024 an engineer integrating zstd
dictionaries at Roblox reported the same signature upstream -- ``a max
dict size of 512KB gets 90x ratio but 550KB gets 14x
ratio''~\cite{zstdissue4127} -- in a thread answered and closed as
completed without the trainer's behavior changing. We do not reproduce
that magnitude (their data is private, well outside the small-record
regime), but the mechanism below accounts for the signature.

\textbf{Mechanism.} Three interacting behaviors, traced to source and
confirmed by instrumented rebuild against
\texttt{facebook/zstd main@82d322c}, produce the collapse.
\textbf{M1:} epoch count scales with the \emph{requested} budget, not
the corpus (\texttt{cover.c:734-749}) -- at $k{=}50$ and a 2\,MiB
request, a 36\,MB training set becomes $\sim$42{,}000 sub-kilobyte
epochs. \textbf{M2:} the build loop exits after a hard-coded run of dead
epochs (fastCOVER: 10, \texttt{fastcover.c:406}), trivially reached on
redundant corpora, leaving the output buffer substantially unfilled.
\textbf{M3 (root cause):} the selection objective scores each candidate
by its \emph{emitted} size plus check-set compressed size
(\texttt{cover.c:899,998}), unable to distinguish ``small because
efficient'' from ``small because starved''; as the request grows, a
starved candidate's score stays pinned while every well-formed
candidate's inflates, so past a threshold the optimizer deterministically
selects the most degenerate build -- confirmed on \texttt{gharchive} at
2\,MiB, where the winner had the \emph{worst} compression term of all
candidates and won purely on a 1.62\,MB size advantage from its own
failed fill. Roughly two thirds of the \texttt{github\_users} collapse
traces to this selection error, one third to genuine oversize dilution.
A minor \textbf{M4} compounds it: finalize shrink-to-fit keeps the
\emph{first} bytes when trimming for the entropy header
(\texttt{zdict.c:902-935}), discarding the tail -- the highest-scoring
content the builder placed nearest the data on purpose
(\S\ref{sec:background-zstd-dicts}). Nobody noticed because both
trainers assign \texttt{ctx->displayLevel} and then \texttt{memset} the
context to zero a few lines later (\texttt{cover.c:639,658};
\texttt{fastcover.c:320,343}), silently suppressing every build-loop
diagnostic at every \texttt{-v} level.

\textbf{Classification.} M2, M3, and \texttt{displayLevel} are
implementation defects; content-exhaustion pinning is inherent;
\texttt{--maxdict} semantics are a documentation gap. We have drafted
four upstream fixes (\S\ref{sec:discussion}): charge the objective by
requested capacity, scale the zero-score tolerance to epoch count, fix
\texttt{displayLevel}, and shrink from the front instead of the back.

\subsection{Size blindness}
\label{sec:diagnosis-size-blindness}

Ratio-vs-budget curves from 2KB to 2MB (Figure~\ref{fig:f2-size-curves},
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) show peaks varying from 32KB to over 2MB
depending on corpus and level; the default 110KB budget loses up to
22.4\% (gharchive, level 19), and the curves are locally non-monotonic.
The trainer searches $(d,k)$ but never size; the shrinkDict path that
would let it respond to size is dead code.

\subsection{Level blindness (three independent confirmations)}
\label{sec:diagnosis-level-blindness}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] Size-response inverts by level: levels 1--3 peak small then
    decline, while level 19's response rises monotonically.
  \item[(b)] Repcode seeding: $+0.3$--$0.4\%$ at level 19,
    $-0.2$--$0.6\%$ at level 1 -- the first proper evaluation of the
    \texttt{\#if 0} path.
  \item[(c)] Refinement: $+1.7$--$6.6\%$ at level 19, approximately 0 at
    level 3.
\end{enumerate}

Mechanism: fast levels index only a suffix (hash overwrite) with greedy
parsers, while \texttt{btopt} indexes everything and cost-models its
choices. The same pattern should recur whenever the consumer is a
different codec entirely -- LZ4's match window, for instance, is capped
at 64\,KiB, so only the tail of a large dictionary is reachable at all --
but we have not measured this cross-codec case in the rebuilt
environment; \S\ref{sec:discussion} flags it as the conceptual
generalization of level blindness rather than as a result.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

We seek
\[
  \min_{D \,:\, |D| \le B} \; \sum_{x} \bigl| \mathrm{zstd}_L(x \mid D) \bigr|,
\]
an NP-hardness heritage traceable to Storer--Szymanski
substitution~\cite{storer1982textual} and smallest-grammar
APX-hardness~\cite{charikar2005smallestgrammar,casel2019grammar}. We
treat this as blackbox optimization with a $\sim$1-second exact oracle
(a real compression run). The conceptual core of the paper follows from
that oracle's cost: evaluation is cheap, so the design question is how
to spend an evaluation budget.

\section{Design: validation-driven, level-aware trainer}
\label{sec:design}

The diagnosis in \S\ref{sec:diagnosis} suggests a design rather than an
algorithm: the stock trainer's failures are failures of
\emph{selection}, not of its covering heuristic, which is sound and
which we retain unchanged. Our trainer searches candidates adjudicated
by measurement -- evaluation is cheap ($\sim$1s/run,
\S\ref{sec:problem-statement}) and directly measures the quantity we
want, so there is no proxy objective. The caller supplies one training
directory, split 85/15 by seed into a \emph{fit} set (what any
underlying trainer sees) and a \emph{validation} set (what every
accept/reject decision is measured on); the user's held-out data is
never opened. This bounds overfitting in our greedy per-stage loops --
the largest fit-to-heldout deviation observed is 0.14\%.

\subsection{Stage 1: size and family search}
\label{sec:design-stage1}

Stage 1 sweeps a geometric size ladder from 2\,KiB to the caller's cap
with both fastCOVER and COVER, keeping whichever candidate scores best
on validation at the target level. \textbf{Floor candidate:} fastCOVER
at $\min(\text{cap}, 110\,\text{KiB})$ -- precisely what \texttt{zstd
--train} would produce -- runs first unconditionally, so every later
decision is a comparison against what the user already had; this makes
the tool never-worse by construction (Proposition~\ref{prop:anytime-floor}).
\textbf{Cheap-first ordering:} fastCOVER sizes run before COVER sizes,
so a truncated budget cuts the expensive tail, not size coverage -- a
size-first ordering instead let large corpora exhaust the budget on
COVER at small sizes, losing to the default by up to 19\%.
\textbf{Degeneracy guard:} emitted size is flagged against requested
size, but since selection is by measured compression, not requested
size (\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-cliff}, M3), a starved build cannot win by
being small. A ladder winner is \textbf{refit on the full training
directory} and kept only if validation does not regress -- without this
the tool loses $\sim$0.1\% to the default whenever no ladder entry beats
the floor.

\subsection{Stages 2 \& 3 (target level $\ge 16$): refinement and repcode seeding}
\label{sec:design-stage2}

Stage 1 chooses \emph{which} dictionary; Stage 2 asks what the covering
heuristic never asks: when real compressions run against it, which
bytes do they reference? We extract the sequence stream from fit-set
compressions (\texttt{ZSTD\_generateSequences}), map matches to the
regions they consumed, evict regions never touched or in the weakest
referenced decile, and refill from the worst-compressing fit files,
compacting proven content toward the tail (\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-cliff},
M4). Each round is kept only if it improves fit-set compressed size,
stopping at the first rejection, and the offset-to-region mapping
self-tests against a planted synthetic region before the stage may run
at all. Literally-dead content is rare (0--4\%), so gains come from
displacing \emph{weakly} referenced content.

\label{sec:design-stage3}
Stage 3 seeds the three recent-offset values the encoder starts each
frame with, which the stock trainer computes and discards, writing
$\{1,4,8\}$ (\S\ref{sec:background-zstd-dicts}): we measure the fit
set's top three offsets and patch them in, keeping the result only if
validation improves -- to our knowledge the first published measurement
of the feature the \texttt{\#if 0} comment in \texttt{zdict.c} left
unevaluated.

\label{sec:design-gating}
Both stages gate on target level $\ge 16$ because
\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-level-blindness} explains why they must: fast
levels index only part of a large dictionary into small hash tables and
parse greedily, so curated content and seeded offsets cannot be
exploited and can actively mislead the parser, while level 19's optimal
parser indexes everything and cost-compares its options. Rather than
average these opposite behaviors, the tool takes target level as an
input and does different work for different answers.

\subsection{Guarantees}
\label{sec:design-guarantees}

\begin{sloppypar}
Let $S$ be the size ladder, $B$ the caller's budget cap, and
$C(B) = \{(t,s) : t \in \{\text{fastCOVER}, \text{COVER}\},\ s \in S,\ s
\le B\} \cup \{\text{floor}(B)\}$ the Stage-1 candidate set, where
$\text{floor}(B) = (\text{fastCOVER}, \min(B, 110\,\text{KiB}))$. The
tool returns $\arg\max_{c \in C(B)} r_{\text{val}}(c)$, the candidate
with the best measured validation-split compression ratio.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{proposition}[Budget monotonicity]
\label{prop:budget-monotonicity}
For $B_1 \le B_2$, $C(B_1) \subseteq C(B_2)$, so the selected validation
ratio is monotone non-decreasing in $B$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Anytime floor]
\label{prop:anytime-floor}
For any time budget sufficient to evaluate $\text{floor}(B)$ -- seconds,
and it is evaluated first -- the selected validation ratio is at least
that of the incumbent trainer at the same cap.
\end{proposition}

Both are immediate from the construction, and cheap-first ordering
preserves both under truncation. Their content is not mathematical
depth but the observation that the \emph{deployed} trainer satisfies
neither: Proposition~\ref{prop:budget-monotonicity} fails empirically by
24--31\% (\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-cliff}), and
Proposition~\ref{prop:anytime-floor} fails because there is no floor at
all -- DCC-normal scope for a guarantee on a systems contribution
(cf.\ Machete~\cite{machete2024}). Two honest limits: the guarantees
hold on the validation measure, not held-out data
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation} quantifies the gap), and
Proposition~\ref{prop:budget-monotonicity} constrains the
\emph{selected} candidate, not the underlying trainers -- a starved
build is still a starved build, we merely decline to select it.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\textbf{Setup.} Five small-record corpora spanning deployment shapes,
including zstd's own benchmark set: \texttt{github\_users} (9{,}114 JSON
user records), \texttt{gharchive} (20{,}000 GitHub events),
\texttt{weblogs} (15{,}000 batches of 20 NASA-HTTP log lines),
\texttt{apijson} (10{,}000 Crossref records), \texttt{csvrows} (10{,}000
50-row NYC 311 CSV fragments), each split 80/20 by seeded shuffle, with
heldout compressed exactly once per configuration. We evaluate at level
3 (RocksDB/ScyllaDB SSTable/Cassandra default) and 19 (archival), plus
level 1 for a cell motivated by ScyllaDB's (off-by-default) RPC path
(\S\ref{sec:background-deployment}), on an Apple M4 against zstd
\texttt{82d322c} (v1.6.0), every dictionary round-trip-verified against
an independent stock binary; x86 replication of throughput is planned
but not run (\S\ref{sec:discussion}). Midway through this work the
machine's temporary storage was cleared, destroying the experimental
tree; we rebuilt it from recorded specifications -- re-fetching every
corpus, recompiling, re-running every experiment -- and report only
rebuilt-environment numbers. Headline behaviors, including the
budget-degeneracy collapse, reproduced on freshly downloaded data with a
fresh split, an unusually strong reproducibility check; a byte-for-byte
determinism check across reruns was \emph{not} repeated post-rebuild and
should be treated as unverified (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Main results}
\label{sec:evaluation-main}

\input{tables/t1_main.tex}

Against the deployed default (\texttt{zstd --train} at its 110\,KiB
budget), our trainer improves compression ratio on every corpus at both
levels (Table~\ref{tab:t1-main}): between +3.1\% and +14.3\% at level 3
and between +11.5\% and +32.1\% at level 19, averaging roughly +8\% and
+23\% respectively. The no-training control -- random training samples
concatenated to the same 110\,KiB -- trails the stock trainer
everywhere, confirming the covering heuristic earns its keep and our
gains are not simply ``any bytes will do.'' These are the numbers a
practitioner would see from replacing their dictionary file and
changing nothing else; \S\ref{sec:evaluation-parity} and
\S\ref{sec:evaluation-cost} take up how much of them is size versus
method.

\subsection{Matched-size and production configurations}
\label{sec:evaluation-parity}

Our trainer chooses its own size, usually larger than the 110\,KiB
default, so we rebuilt the baseline at \emph{our} winning size for every
corpus and level (full breakdown: Table~\ref{tab:t4-parity},
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). At level 3, almost none of the headline
advantage survives -- two corpora match exactly, two retain +0.2\%/+1.5\%,
so 0--11\% of the gain is method rather than size. At level 19 a real
margin remains: +6.2\%/+6.9\%/+2.8\% (\texttt{github\_users}/\texttt{weblogs}/\texttt{csvrows})
at identical budgets. This defines the contribution: at fast levels the
trainer \emph{finds} the right size safely and automatically, a size the
stock trainer could in principle also have produced; at high levels
refinement and seeding add compression no choice of budget recovers.
Three of ten matched-size cells could not be run as intended -- the
stock trainer, asked for our winning budget, itself emitted
substantially less (e.g.\ 892\,KiB against a 2\,MiB request on
\texttt{gharchive} L19) -- so the size confound is not fully separable
from \S\ref{sec:diagnosis-cliff}: part of ``we used a bigger dictionary''
is ``we could use a bigger dictionary.''

Reproducing Cassandra's shipped default directly and two further
deployment-motivated settings (\S\ref{sec:background-deployment}): at
Cassandra's 64\,KiB level-3 budget we beat the stock trainer on three
corpora (+0.4\% to +1.7\%) and tie exactly on two, no losses -- ties
occur where no ladder candidate beats the incumbent-equivalent floor,
so the tool returns the dictionary the user already had, the anytime
guarantee (\S\ref{sec:design-guarantees}) operating as designed. At a
level-1, 110\,KiB setting -- the budget and level ScyllaDB's RPC path
would use if enabled, though that path defaults to off -- we win on all
five corpora, +1.6\% to +8.1\%. RocksDB's opt-in, non-default
no-trainer fallback ranks between no dictionary and the stock trainer
in nine of ten cells and trails our pipeline by a median of 18\%.

\subsection{Alternative trainers and ablation}
\label{sec:evaluation-baselines}

\texttt{klauspost/compress}'s \texttt{builddict}~\cite{klauspost}, the
closest prior art to our level-awareness claim, loses to the stock zstd
default on all twenty cells we ran (Table~\ref{tab:t3-baselines},
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}), emitting $\sim$17\,KiB on
\texttt{github\_users} regardless of a 1\,MiB request; we report this
without enthusiasm -- it should have been a harder baseline to beat.

\label{sec:evaluation-ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:t1-main}'s \emph{tuned} column is Stage 1 alone;
\emph{full} adds Stages 2--3, which contribute +1.0 to +9.0 percentage
points at level 19, positive on all five corpora. At level 3, where
Stages 2--3 are gated off (\S\ref{sec:design-gating}), \emph{tuned} and
\emph{full} should coincide exactly; they instead differ by 0\% to
$-2.7\%$, because the two variants are independently retrained rather
than one extending the other (\texttt{tools/run\_campaign.py}) -- Stage
1's own run-to-run variance, and evidence that byte-identical
reproducibility was not independently confirmed post-rebuild
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Equal compute, cost, and unmeasured claims}
\label{sec:evaluation-equalcompute}

\begin{sloppypar}
Our trainer spends minutes to tens of minutes where \texttt{zstd
--train} spends 0.2--3.2\,s (\texttt{results/benchmark\_v2.csv}). The
corresponding manual control gives the incumbent our wall-clock budget
to sweep sizes, selecting by measured validation compression
(Table~\ref{tab:t2-equalcompute}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}): this
closes the gap at level 3 (1.5\% above to 9.5\% below our result, a
wash) while at level 19 we still win on four of five corpora, +1.6\% to
+8.5\%, losing narrowly on \texttt{apijson} ($-1.1\%$). The sweep also
reproduced the budget-degeneracy pathology -- several large-budget
candidates emitted dictionaries 25--30\% worse than at half the budget,
surviving only because our harness selects by measured compression, not
requested size (\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-cliff}, M3) -- the clearest
demonstration that the safety property in
\S\ref{sec:design-guarantees} matters: the naive version of this
experiment silently selects the broken dictionary. Peak-RSS on
\texttt{github\_users}/\texttt{gharchive} shows batch-compression memory
essentially unaffected by dictionary choice (2.8--6.0\,MB).
\label{sec:evaluation-cost}
\end{sloppypar}

Compression/decompression throughput, bootstrap confidence intervals
over the heldout deltas, and cross-codec transfer to Brotli and LZ4 were
designed and partially run before the storage wipe
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation-main}) but not re-executed against the rebuilt
corpora, and \texttt{results/benchmark\_v2.csv} contains no post-rebuild
rows for any of them. \todo{Re-run throughput, a paired bootstrap over
the main-results heldout deltas, and Brotli/LZ4 cross-codec transfer in
the rebuilt environment before submission; report honestly if any
pre-wipe number fails to reproduce.} We do not restate pre-wipe figures
here: a number appears only once recomputed against the rebuilt
pipeline (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\textbf{Dictionary construction.} The trainer descends from Liao, Petri,
Moffat and Wirth's relative Lempel-Ziv dictionary
construction~\cite{liao2016cover}, a covering problem over sampled
substrings; zstd adopted it as \texttt{--train-cover} and later added
the fastCOVER approximation that is today's CLI default, unchanged
since 2018. fastCOVER itself has no academic description at all: it
entered zstd via pull requests \#1250 and \#1274 (2018), not a paper.
We reuse the covering heuristic unmodified; our contribution
is the selection layer around it. That paper has on the order of a
dozen citations against hundreds of vendored copies, absorbed into
deployments under a feature-flag name it never used. Level-dependence
itself is not a new observation: a zstd maintainer noted in 2019 that
training at the level you actually compress at ``will tune the
dictionary better''~\cite{zstdissue1572}, and a 2022 request to expose
a level parameter was declined for ABI
stability~\cite{zstdissue3213}. Our claim is the \emph{mechanism}
(three independent, opposite-sign inversions traced to hash-table
overwrite vs.\ full indexing, \S\ref{sec:diagnosis-level-blindness})
and \emph{magnitude} -- including, to our knowledge, the first
measurement of the disabled repcode-seeding path -- not the base
observation. \texttt{klauspost/compress}~\cite{klauspost}
is the only other tool we know of that emits standard-format zstd
dictionaries and exposes a level-targeting flag, anticipating our
level-awareness argument; we benchmark it directly
(Table~\ref{tab:t3-baselines}) and it loses to the stock default on
every cell we ran. Brotli's ``durchschlag'' dictionary
generator~\cite{rfc9841,alakuijala2019brotli} is a related covering
method for raw-content dictionaries; cross-codec transfer to it is
designed but not yet re-executed post-rebuild
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation-cost}), so we cite rather than benchmark it.

\textbf{RLZ, methodology, and theory.} Our refinement stage
(\S\ref{sec:design-stage2}) adapts RLZ reference pruning and
selection~\cite{hoobin2011rlz,puglisi2023rlz}: the novelty is doing it
against the production encoder's own sequence stream, at the granularity
that encoder matches on, gated by measured compressed size, plus the
finding that literally-dead content is rare (0--4\%). Methodologically we
follow recent practical compression work that measures precisely why
the incumbent underperforms, then designs against the
measurement~\cite{boncz2020fsst,afroozeh2024alp,kuschewski2023btrblocks,xia2016fastcdc},
reporting where we lose
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation-parity}--\ref{sec:evaluation-equalcompute}) as
well as where we win; domain-specific compression~\cite{machete2024} and
LZ77 acceleration~\cite{lzhv2025} are recent DCC neighbors in a
different corner of the space. Theoretically, optimal dictionary choice
is intractable in general -- NP-complete~\cite{storer1982textual},
APX-hard as smallest-grammar~\cite{charikar2005smallestgrammar,casel2019grammar}
(\S\ref{sec:problem-statement}) -- so our propositions
(\S\ref{sec:design-guarantees}) deliberately constrain only the
\emph{selection} rule; Niesen's deduplication
analysis~\cite{niesen2019dedup} treats a related but distinct question,
not construction for a specific encoder.

\textbf{Parameter tuning and deployment.} \texttt{zstd}'s
\texttt{paramgrill} and Meta's managed-compression
blog~\cite{metaengineering2018zstd} disclose unpublished per-workload
tuning -- adjacent to us (it tunes the encoder, we build the dictionary)
but evidence that per-workload specialization is known practice,
unpublished science. OpenZL~\cite{openzl}, training format-specific
compression graphs, is the heavyweight successor direction, its
dictionary story left to its own future work. Deployment status varies
by system and path (\S\ref{sec:background-deployment}): ScyllaDB's
SSTable path and, opt-in, RocksDB use trained dictionaries in
production; Cassandra's CEP-54 targets the same but is unreleased; and
ScyllaDB's own RPC-dictionary path is off by default. The web has
standardized dictionary transport~\cite{rfc9842}, but Chrome's own
use-counters put overall usage at roughly 0.09\% of page loads and its
zstd variant two orders of magnitude smaller
still~\cite{chromestatususecounters}, and Cloudflare's implementation is
a passthrough beta that itself generates no
dictionaries~\cite{cloudflaresharedddict}. This variation is why
\S\ref{sec:evaluation-parity} matches these specific configurations
rather than only our own chosen sizes.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Corpus- and level-dependence.} Table~\ref{tab:t1-main}'s spread --
+3.1\% to +14.3\% at level 3, +11.5\% to +32.1\% at level 19 -- is real
variation, driven by how redundant a corpus is and how much of that
redundancy the default 110\,KiB budget already captures. Read it as a
range, not an average.

\textbf{Fast levels: safe automation, not new compression.} Rebuilding
the stock trainer at our winning size leaves 0--11\% of the level-3
headline gain (\S\ref{sec:evaluation-parity}); the fixed-budget
Cassandra-64\,KiB cells confirm this without a size confound (0.4--1.7\%
or tie, never lose), and an equal-wall-clock sweep
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation-equalcompute}) closes the level-3 gap entirely.
The fast-level contribution is that the tool \emph{finds} the right size
and never regresses (Proposition~\ref{prop:anytime-floor}), not that it
discovers content the incumbent could not in principle have found. At
level 19 a real margin survives both controls, because refinement and
repcode seeding exploit which bytes real compressions reference --
information no choice of budget recovers.

\textbf{Unverified, pending re-measurement.} All numbers here are
single-machine (Apple M4); throughput, bootstrap CIs, and cross-codec
transfer were designed pre-wipe but not re-run against the rebuilt
corpora, so we report none rather than reuse figures we could not
recompute (\S\ref{sec:evaluation-cost}). Byte-identical reproducibility
across reruns, an assumption of the design, was likewise not re-checked;
\S\ref{sec:evaluation-ablation}'s small, mixed-sign \emph{tuned}/\emph{full}
deltas at level 3 are indirect evidence it may not hold exactly.
Held-out files within a corpus are also shuffled draws from one scrape,
not independent observations, so a bootstrap over them would understate
true variance -- one more reason we read Table~\ref{tab:t1-main} as five
point estimates, not a population sample, and attached no intervals we
could not first re-measure honestly. Separately, asking the stock
trainer for our winning budget starved it on three matched-size cells
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation-parity}) -- the defect under study, not a clean
control -- so we excluded them from the survival-percentage summary
rather than average them in; that the incumbent sometimes cannot be
given the size we use is itself evidence for \S\ref{sec:diagnosis-cliff}.

\textbf{Future work and upstreaming.} Sharing Stage 1's search across
levels and a speed-constrained mode trading ratio for a bounded
training/decompression budget are natural next steps given the
41\,s--59\,min training cost and level-19 throughput cost of large
dictionaries. We have drafted, but not submitted, four upstream fixes
(\S\ref{sec:diagnosis-cliff}): charge the objective by requested rather
than emitted capacity, scale the zero-score tolerance, fix
\texttt{displayLevel}, and shrink from the front. A more general,
ML-guided scoring loop, not the greedy round-by-round acceptance used
here, is future work.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Diagnosis-first beats algorithm-first: a $\sim$1-second exact oracle
changes the design space. A 2016 covering heuristic, unmodified, plus a
validation-driven selection layer around it yields double-digit archival
gains, gains that survive matched-size and equal-compute scrutiny, and
guaranteed-safe defaults at every budget -- while the safe-automation
story at fast levels is, on its own honest terms, still worth shipping.
