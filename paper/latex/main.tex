\begin{abstract}
Zstandard's dictionary trainer (COVER/fastCOVER, WWW 2016)~\cite{liao2016cover}
is the de-facto standard for small-record compression, deployed in
RocksDB, ScyllaDB, and the emerging shared-dictionary web
ecosystem~\cite{rfc9842} -- yet it has received no algorithmic attention
since 2018 despite documented pathological failures. We present the
first mechanistic diagnosis of its behavior on real corpora, identifying
three defects: (1) \emph{budget degeneracy} -- past internal caps,
requesting a larger dictionary silently yields a smaller one, collapsing
compression ratio by up to 28\% in step-function drops on zstd's own
benchmark corpus (consistent with, though smaller than, a well-known
unresolved issue report~\cite{zstdissue4127}); (2) \emph{size blindness}
-- the trainer never searches dictionary size, though the optimal size
varies by two orders of magnitude across corpora and the default budget
leaves 2--22\% compression on the table; (3) \emph{level blindness} --
construction decisions (content selection, size response, repcode
seeding) have opposite effects at fast versus high compression levels,
yet the trainer targets a single level. Guided by this diagnosis, we
build a validation-driven trainer that searches size and trainer family
under a target-level objective with an anytime never-worse guarantee,
refines dictionary content by measuring which regions real compressions
reference, and seeds repeat-offset codes -- each stage validation-gated.
On five real corpora spanning JSON records, event streams, server logs,
and tabular data, our trainer improves compression ratio over
\texttt{zstd --train} defaults by 1.8--13.8\% at level 3 and 6.9--32.3\%
at level 19; the dictionaries also transfer to Brotli's raw-dictionary
mode (+2--27\% over the incumbent trainer's). Output is
100\%-standard-format, usable by every deployed zstd. Tool and all
experiments are open source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Dictionaries are how zstd compresses small data, and small data is
where modern systems live: key-value stores, logs, telemetry, and the
emerging shared-dictionary web transport~\cite{rfc9842}. One trainer
serves all of it; it is a 2016 heuristic~\cite{liao2016cover}, frozen
since 2018, whose maintainer states that the ideal dictionary builder
``does not exist.''

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The first mechanistic quantitative diagnosis of the incumbent
    trainer, comprising the budget-degeneracy root cause, size
    blindness, and level blindness, including the likely root cause of
    issue \#4127~\cite{zstdissue4127}.
  \item A level-aware, validation-driven trainer with an anytime-safety
    guarantee, with each component individually ablated.
  \item A 5-corpus, 2-level, 7-variant benchmark, including a Brotli
    generator, klauspost \texttt{builddict}~\cite{klauspost}, an
    equal-compute stock sweep, and no-training controls; plus
    cross-codec transfer to Brotli.
  \item A layout-sensitivity observation (fast-level ratios swing
    5--16\% under content permutation; level 19 barely moves), with
    honest negative results for naive layout optimization.
  \item An open-source tool that emits 100\%-standard-format
    dictionaries.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Size blindness}
\label{sec:diagnosis-size-blindness}

Ratio-vs-budget curves from 2KB to 2MB show peaks varying from 32KB to
over 2MB depending on corpus and level; the default 110KB budget loses
up to 22.4\% (gharchive, level 19), and the curves are locally

\subsection{Level blindness (three independent confirmations)}
\label{sec:diagnosis-level-blindness}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] Size-response inverts by level: levels 1--3 peak small then
    decline, while level 19's response rises monotonically.
  \item[(b)] Repcode seeding: $+0.3$--$0.4\%$ at level 19,
    $-0.2$--$0.6\%$ at level 1 -- the first proper evaluation of the
    \texttt{\#if 0} path.
  \item[(c)] Refinement: $+1.7$--$6.6\%$ at level 19, approximately 0 at
    level 3.
\end{enumerate}

Mechanism: fast levels index only a suffix (hash overwrite) with greedy
parsers, while \texttt{btopt} indexes everything and cost-models its
choices. A third, cross-codec instance of the same pattern: LZ4's 64KB
window makes our size-searched dictionaries mis-fit -- only
matched-size content wins -- so construction must know its consumer.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

We seek
\[
  \min_{D \,:\, |D| \le B} \; \sum_{x} \bigl| \mathrm{zstd}_L(x \mid D) \bigr|,
\]
an NP-hardness heritage traceable to Storer--Szymanski
substitution~\cite{storer1982textual} and smallest-grammar
APX-hardness~\cite{charikar2005smallestgrammar,casel2019grammar}. We
treat this as blackbox optimization with a $\sim$1-second exact oracle
(a real compression run). The conceptual core of the paper follows from
that oracle's cost: evaluation is cheap, so the design question is how
to spend an evaluation budget.

\subsection{Guarantees}
\label{sec:design-guarantees}

\begin{proposition}[Budget monotonicity]
\label{prop:budget-monotonicity}
If the candidate set satisfies $C(B_1) \subseteq C(B_2)$ for
$B_1 \le B_2$, then the selected validation ratio is monotone
non-decreasing in $B$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Anytime floor]
\label{prop:anytime-floor}
Any time budget covering the floor candidate implies the selected
dictionary is at least incumbent-equivalent.
\end{proposition}

Cheap-first ordering preserves Proposition~\ref{prop:budget-monotonicity}
under truncation (the fastcover sub-ladder completes in
$\sim$10 seconds). By contrast, the stock optimizer violates
monotonicity empirically ($-20$ to $-28\%$ measured, \S3.1). These
guarantees hold on the validation measure; heldout deviation is bounded
by selection noise (measured $\le 0.14\%$).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related-work}

COVER (WWW 2016)~\cite{liao2016cover} has 16 citations against 607
vendored copies -- absorbed into deployments nameless. klauspost
\texttt{builddict}~\cite{klauspost} exposes a \texttt{-zlevel} flag,
prior art for level-targeting, with parity claimed at best; we cite and
benchmark it directly (Table~\ref{tab:t3-baselines}). Content-defined
chunking work (FastCDC~\cite{xia2016fastcdc}) and string/columnar
compressors (FSST~\cite{boncz2020fsst}, ALP~\cite{afroozeh2024alp},
BtrBlocks~\cite{kuschewski2023btrblocks}) bound the broader
small-record/columnar compression space we sit adjacent to, alongside
recent DCC dictionary-adjacent work (Machete~\cite{machete2024},
LZHV~\cite{lzhv2025}). Brotli's ``durchschlag'' dictionary generator and
format~\cite{rfc9841,alakuijala2019brotli} is the cross-codec transfer
target in \S\ref{sec:evaluation-cross-codec}. The Relative Lempel-Ziv
line~\cite{hoobin2011rlz,puglisi2023rlz} positions our refinement stage
(\S\ref{sec:design-stage2}) as an adaptation of RLZ pruning ideas.
\texttt{zstd}'s own \texttt{--paramgrill} tuner and Meta's managed-
compression engineering blog~\cite{metaengineering2018zstd} disclose
parameter tuning that was never formally published. OpenZL~\cite{openzl}
is a heavyweight alternative direction worth contrasting against.
