## 6.0 Setup

Five corpora of small records, chosen to span the shapes dictionaries are
deployed against and to include the corpus zstd itself publishes for this
purpose: `github_users` (9,114 JSON user records, zstd's own benchmark sample
set), `gharchive` (20,000 GitHub event objects), `weblogs` (15,000 batches of
20 NASA-HTTP access-log lines), `apijson` (10,000 Crossref publication
records), and `csvrows` (10,000 50-row CSV fragments from NYC 311 service
requests). Each is split 80/20 into training and held-out sets by seeded
shuffle. Training touches only the training set; the held-out set is
compressed exactly once per configuration reported.

We evaluate at compression level 3 — the default for RocksDB (opt-in),
ScyllaDB SSTables, and Cassandra's own code default — and level 19,
representing write-once archival use; level 1 appears in a parity cell
motivated by ScyllaDB's RPC compression path, which is off by default
(§2.3) — that cell should not be read as a production claim. All measurements were
made on an Apple M4 (10 cores) against zstd built from `82d322c` (v1.6.0),
with round-trip correctness of every dictionary verified against an
independently installed stock zstd binary.

A note on provenance. Midway through this work the machine's temporary
storage was cleared, destroying the entire experimental tree. We rebuilt it
from the recorded specifications — re-fetching every corpus from its original
source, recompiling the toolchain, and re-running every experiment — and
report only the numbers from the rebuilt environment. The headline behaviours
reproduced, including the budget-degeneracy collapse, on freshly downloaded
data with a fresh split. We note this because it is an unusually strong form
of the reproducibility check papers normally promise and rarely get to
perform.

## 6.1 Main results

Against the deployed default (`zstd --train` at its 110 KiB default budget),
our trainer improves compression ratio on every corpus at both levels
(Table~\ref{tab:main}): between +3.1% and +14.3% at level 3, and between
+11.5% and +32.1% at level 19, averaging roughly +8% and +23% respectively.
The no-training control — random training samples concatenated to the same
110 KiB — trails the stock trainer everywhere, confirming that the covering
heuristic earns its keep and that our gains are not simply "any bytes will
do."

These are the numbers a practitioner would experience by replacing their
dictionary file and changing nothing else. They are also, on their own, a
misleading basis for judging the method, for two reasons we take up
immediately.

## 6.2 How much of the gain is size? (matched-size comparison)

Our trainer chooses its own dictionary size, and it usually chooses one far
larger than the 110 KiB default — so a fair reviewer will ask how much of
§6.1 is method and how much is merely budget. We therefore rebuilt the
baseline at *our* winning size for every corpus and level
(Table~\ref{tab:parity}).

At level 3, almost none of the advantage survives: on `github_users` and
`csvrows` the matched-size baseline equals our result exactly, and on
`gharchive` and `weblogs` we retain +0.2% and +1.5%. Between 0% and 11% of
the headline level-3 gain is attributable to the method rather than the size.
At level 19 a real margin remains: +6.2% on `github_users`, +6.9% on
`weblogs`, +2.8% on `csvrows` at identical byte budgets — between 10% and 54%
of the headline gain, with the balance again explained by size.

We state this plainly because it defines what the contribution is. At fast
levels, our trainer's value is that it *finds* the right size safely and
automatically; the dictionary it produces is one the stock trainer could also
have produced, had the user known which budget to ask for and been able to
ask for it safely. At high levels, the refinement and seeding stages
contribute compression that no choice of budget recovers.

Three of the ten matched-size cells could not be run as intended, and the
reason is itself a result: asked for the budget our trainer used, the stock
trainer emitted substantially less (892 KiB against 2 MiB requested on
`gharchive` at level 19; 952 KiB against 1 MiB on `apijson`). The baseline in
those cells is not size-matched but size-starved, so we exclude them from the
survival percentages above and report them separately. That the incumbent
sometimes *cannot be given* the size we use is a direct consequence of §3.1,
and it means the size confound is not entirely separable from the defect: part
of "we used a bigger dictionary" is "we could use a bigger dictionary."

## 6.3 Equal compute

Our trainer spends minutes where `zstd --train` spends seconds. The
corresponding control is to give the incumbent our wall-clock budget and let
it sweep dictionary sizes, selecting by measured compression on a validation
split — precisely the manual procedure a diligent engineer would follow.

At level 3 this control closes the gap: across the five corpora it lands
between 1.5% above and 9.5% below our result, i.e. a wash. At level 19 our
trainer still wins on four of five corpora, by +1.6% to +8.5%, losing
narrowly on `apijson` (−1.1%). The conclusion mirrors §6.2 and we draw it in
the same direction: at fast levels we are automating something a careful
engineer could do by hand, and at high levels we are doing something they
could not.

The sweep also reproduced the pathology it was meant to control for: several
of its candidates at large budgets emitted degenerate dictionaries with ratios
25–30% below the same trainer's output at half the budget. The sweep survived
only because our harness selects by measured compression rather than by
requested size — the same discipline the stock optimizer lacks (§3.2, M3).
This is the clearest available demonstration of why the safety property in
§5.5 matters: the naive version of this experiment silently selects the
broken dictionary.

## 6.4 Production configurations and deployment-motivated settings

Deployments do not use our sizes or our levels, so we reproduced one shipped
default directly and two further settings motivated by (but not identical
to) real deployments (Table~\ref{tab:parity}; see §2.3 for the verified
deployment status behind each label).

*Cassandra* trains 64 KiB dictionaries at level 3 — this is the code's own
shipped default (`CompressionDictionaryTrainingConfig`), even though CEP-54
as a whole is not yet merged or released. Constrained to that budget, our
trainer beats the stock trainer on three corpora (+0.4% to +1.7%) and ties
exactly on two — no losses. On `github_users` it wins while selecting a
16 KiB dictionary, a quarter of the permitted budget.

*A level-1, 110 KiB setting motivated by ScyllaDB's RPC path* — that path
(`rpc_dict_training_when`) is off by default, so this cell should be read as
an evaluation datapoint at a level ScyllaDB's RPC path would use if enabled,
not a production-usage claim. There we win on all five corpora, +1.6% to
+8.1%. (ScyllaDB's SSTable-dictionary path, by contrast, is production and,
as of release 2025.4, the *default* SSTable compressor — but its 110 KiB,
level-3 configuration is already covered by the "deployed default" cells in
Table~\ref{tab:main}, so it does not need a separate parity row here.)

*RocksDB* offers an opt-in no-trainer path (dictionaries are off by default;
`max_dict_bytes=0`) that hands raw sampled blocks to
`ZDICT_finalizeDictionary`. It ranks between no dictionary and the stock
trainer in nine of ten cells (median −6.9% against the trainer) and trails our
pipeline by a median of 18%.

The Cassandra ties deserve comment. They occur where no candidate on our
ladder beats the incumbent-equivalent floor, so the tool returns the floor
candidate — retrained on the full training set — and the result is the same
dictionary the user already had. This is the anytime guarantee of §5.5
operating as designed: the honest outcome of a search that found nothing
better is to return what you started with, at a cost of a few minutes, rather
than something worse.

## 6.5 Alternative trainers

`klauspost/compress`'s `builddict` is the only other tool we are aware of that
emits standard-format zstd dictionaries, and it exposes a compression-level
flag, making it the closest prior art to our level-awareness claim. It loses
to the stock zstd default in all twenty corpus-level cells we ran. On
`github_users` it emits roughly 17 KiB regardless of the requested budget —
1.6% of a 1 MiB request. Brotli's `dictionary_generator` is likewise not
competitive in the raw-content setting. We report these results without
enthusiasm: they are baselines that should have been harder to beat, and their
weakness is part of why the lane was open.
